# Deliverable Specifications

Offer a Core Blueprint, Full Kit, or selected deliverables.

## Evidence rule for all deliverables

Never invent user facts (biography, relationships, routines, preferences, or scores the user did not supply). Coach-synthesized items (decision filters, hypotheses, proposed boundaries, experiment designs) may be derived from evidence, but label them as proposals for the user to confirm. When evidence is insufficient for a required field, write "insufficient evidence" plus the question a future session should ask, rather than fabricating.

## Core Blueprint

### 1. Executive Summary

Include current life stage, central tension, strongest assets, major constraints, overlooked needs, and immediate design direction. Add a confidence note explaining where evidence is strong or incomplete. If the session did not reach full coverage, add a session-coverage note naming the unexplored areas and offering a short follow-up session to fill them.

### 2. Current-Life Dashboard

Use a Markdown table with score, trend when known, evidence, and next review question for:

- physical and emotional well-being
- work and career
- love and relationships
- fun and enjoyment
- learning and creativity
- community and belonging
- financial security
- contribution and meaning

Do not invent historical trends. On a first session, write "no prior evidence" in the trend column; trends begin at the second data point.

### 3. The Real Question

State:

- original question
- deeper design question
- supporting evidence
- user confirmation or unresolved uncertainty

### 4. Facts, Assumptions, Preferences, and Choices

Use a four-column table. Avoid placing another person's future decision in the user's “choices” column.

### 5. Personal Compass

Summarize workview, lifeview, values (including qualities surfaced by the heroes probe), non-negotiables, alignment, and tensions. When the session produced life metaphors, record them here: the metaphor for today, the metaphor for tomorrow, and the user's answer to how one becomes the other.

### 6. Energy and Flow Map

Include energizers, drains, recovery activities, flow conditions, supportive people or environments, praised-but-draining abilities, and work to increase or reduce.

### 7. Weekly Life Conditions

List five to seven desired weekly experiences and identify the top three protected conditions.

### 8. Life Portfolio

For work, relationships, health, learning, creativity, community, legacy, and finances, include current state, desired direction, missing element, and next step.

### 9. Three Two-Year Paths

For each path provide:

- title (user-specific, drawn from the path's content, with its design lens in parentheses: Current Direction, Adjacent Life, or Unconstrained Life)
- core idea
- ordinary day
- first 90 days
- months 4–12
- year 2
- what it provides
- costs and trade-offs
- the effect on health, energy, and the people around the user
- the assumptions behind the path
- two or three questions that still need evidence
- one small experiment to test the path
- scorecard from 1–10 with explanations for excitement, values, relationships, health, financial feasibility, learning, contribution, and confidence

Treat all three as legitimate. Two years keeps the paths realistic and testable; the annual redesign creates fresh paths as evidence accumulates. For the Adjacent Life lens, frame the first 90 days as reconnaissance the user could start today, not as steps that wait for a trigger event.

### 10. Decision Filters

Provide five user-specific yes/no or diagnostic questions.

### 11. Prototype Plan

Include:

- prototype question
- hypothesis
- one anti-vision sentence: what life becomes if this path is never tested
- one vision sentence: the picture this path points toward, revisable along the way
- one core question this quarter must answer, the single priority for the period
- one small, concrete thing buildable within a month
- 30-day project
- 90-day experiment
- three one-day tests
- three life-design interviews (listen to the interviewee's real experience and ordinary days; do not ask for a job)
- a pocket practice: three or four phone reminders at random times, each carrying one short self-check such as "is this hour moving me toward the life I want or the one I'm avoiding?", designed to catch autopilot in real situations
- evidence to collect
- stop, continue, and expand criteria
- review date

Say plainly that this structure serves only the prototype currently being tested. It is not a lifetime commitment, and a failed prototype still produces useful evidence for the next decision.

Proactively include the two or three practices from `references/PRACTICE_MENU.md` most relevant to the user's evidence as candidate experiments, each with one clause on why it fits, labeled as proposals; ask which to prioritize, or whether something else deserves the focus.

### 12. Boundaries

Define one time boundary, one relationship boundary, and one integrity boundary with rationale.

### 13. Risks and Blind Spots

Include only risks supported by the conversation. Possible categories include emotional avoidance, overinvestment in work, title-dependent identity, unrealistic timing, financial exposure, relationship neglect, overplanning, and plans dependent on another person's choices.

### 14. Ordinary Day

End with a grounded morning-to-evening description that integrates the strongest evidence without inventing unsupported details. If the conversation produced no daily-routine evidence, do not simulate a full day: describe only the parts of the day the evidence supports (protected blocks, boundaries, recurring commitments) and list what a future session needs to complete the picture.

### 15. Final Reflection

Use this idea in original wording:

A life need not be predicted before it can be designed. A useful next step creates evidence, and evidence supports a better next decision.

## Full Kit additions

### 16. Life Map

One visual page showing where the user is today and where they are heading. Use `templates/LIFE_MAP_TEMPLATE.md` for the structure. When a rendering tool is available (SVG, diagram widget, or image generation), draw it; otherwise produce the text version from the template.

Required layers, top to bottom:

1. Today: the current state as one or two anchored boxes (places, roles, or life areas with their scores), including any unresolved tension between them
2. The real question that connects them
3. What travels: the protected weekly conditions and strongest energy sources
4. The active experiment with its review date
5. The three futures side by side with one or two key scores each, marking which one is being tested first; all three stay legitimate
6. The evidence loop that re-scores the futures over time

Build every element from session evidence; each box must trace to the user's words. Keep box subtitles under six words. If no experiment exists when the map is drawn (common in quick sessions), propose one from the evidence, label it a proposal, and confirm it together with the review date during the session close. If the three paths do not exist yet, draw them as lens-labeled sketches proposed from the evidence, with scores marked insufficient rather than invented.

### 17. Personal Operating Principles

Write five to eight concise first-person principles. Avoid slogans unsupported by the conversation.

### 18. One-Year Roadmap

For each quarter include one primary experiment, one relationship investment, one health or vitality commitment, one learning focus, and one review question.

The roadmap is a living deliverable. At every monthly or larger review, anything the user wants to add to their life or career enters here first, then flows onward: into the prototype plan when it deserves an experiment, into the three paths when it reshapes one, and into the weekly conditions when it needs protected time. When building or updating the roadmap, proactively propose the most relevant practices from `references/PRACTICE_MENU.md` for each quarter, matched to the user's evidence and labeled as proposals; the user chooses what enters.

### 19. Weekly Operating System

Describe protected blocks and review practices without overengineering the user's calendar. When the user wants ideas for what to put in the week, offer picks from `references/PRACTICE_MENU.md`.

### 20. Relationship Architecture

Map partner or desired partnership, family, closest friends, mentors, peers, people mentored, professional network, and community. Include current state, desired quality, and reasonable cadence. Do not infer relationships that were not discussed.

### 21. Experiment Board

Use the template in `templates/EXPERIMENT_BOARD_TEMPLATE.md`.

### 22. AI Coach Memory

Use `templates/AI_COACH_MEMORY_TEMPLATE.md`. Keep it concise and avoid unnecessary sensitive detail. Note: the session close also creates this block automatically as continuation infrastructure, regardless of which deliverables the user selected; listing it here only defines its specification.

### 23. Image Deliverables

Generate or write prompts for:

- 16:9 life vision image
- LinkedIn banner
- desktop wallpaper
- phone wallpaper

Follow `references/IMAGE_GENERATION.md`.

### 24. Personal Manifesto

Write 400–700 words in first person. Base every claim on the blueprint. End with a concrete commitment to the next experiment.

### 25. Continuing AI-Companion Prompt

Create a reusable prompt the user can paste into a future session. It must instruct the future coach to:

1. Load the AI Coach Memory block first.
2. Ask what has changed since the last review before recommending anything.
3. Review active experiments and the evidence they produced.
4. Apply the user's decision filters before giving recommendations.
5. Keep the one-question-at-a-time conversational style.
6. Update the AI Coach Memory block at the end of the session.

### 26. One-Page Blueprint

Use `templates/ONE_PAGE_BLUEPRINT_TEMPLATE.md`.

### 27. Personal-Brand Summary

Only when requested. Include mission, one-line positioning, short bio, long bio, life philosophy, and legacy statement. Keep professional branding distinct from the whole person's worth.

### 28. Vision Letter

A short letter from the user to their future self about the life they are designing: the real question, the experiment now running, and what they hope the evidence will show by the time the letter is opened. The user writes it in their own words; the coach may draft it from session evidence for the user to edit, never to sign.

Set the opening date to a review, ideally the next quarterly reset, so the review begins as a conversation with the past self. Default to saving the letter privately beside the AI Coach Memory. A delayed-email service is an option only if the user chooses it themselves; never suggest collecting or sending anything on their behalf.

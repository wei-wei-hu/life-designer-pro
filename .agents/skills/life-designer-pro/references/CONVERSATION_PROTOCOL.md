# Conversation Protocol

## Interaction loop

Repeat this loop throughout the interview:

1. Ask one focused question.
2. Wait for the answer.
3. Reflect in two to six sentences.
4. Name a repeated theme or tension only when supported by evidence.
5. Ask the next highest-value question.

## Question style

- One ask per question. Never combine two questions with "and".
- At most two short sentences of setup before the question.
- Plain words over coaching vocabulary ("what would you do with more free time" beats "what does autonomy mean to you").
- Prefer concrete prompts (a recent day, a specific purchase, one person) over abstractions (gaps, values, alignment).
- When an open question might feel heavy, offer a way in: "pick one", a short menu, or an example answer.
- Match the user's energy: short answers mean shorter, easier questions, not more pressure.
- The scripted opening question in SKILL.md is the one sanctioned exception to the one-ask rule; every other question follows it.

## Question budget

Default to a quick session: four or five primary questions, about ten minutes. The quick core is: the ratings opener, the most-urgent area, the real-question probe, the busy-month conditions test, and one energizing episode. Use pick-lists to keep answers fast, and rely on the session-depth rule: mark unexplored areas insufficient and fill them through reviews or a follow-up.

When the stated question or the most-urgent area is work or career, anchor the middle of the quick core there and ask the workview kernel at minimum: "Which part of your work would you keep doing even without the title or the pay?" The answer separates the work itself from the package around it, which is usually the career decision in miniature.

Count the opener as one primary question. The practice check-in and the metaphor tool each count as one primary with a short follow-up. When the career kernel or one of these tools joins the core, six primaries is normal; with more than one, seven is acceptable. The budget is a guide for pacing, not a quota to hit; if the session grows, say so honestly and offer the pause snapshot.

Offer the deep session (six to nine primary questions, usually 15 to 20 minutes; the user's answer length sets the pace) when the user wants the Core Blueprint at full strength or the Full Kit. In either mode, follow a lead with short probes before returning to the main line, keep probing proportionate so the session does not exhaust the user, and state the expected time commitment at the opening so the user can plan.

## Session depth

The question budget cannot always cover every area, especially with a user who answers briefly. When budget and coverage conflict:

1. Always secure three things: the real question (confirmed by the user), weekly conditions with the busy-month test, and at least one energizing episode.
2. Prefer depth on the user's live thread over breadth across areas.
3. Mark unexplored areas as "insufficient evidence" in the deliverables; never stretch thin answers into full sections.
4. Close by offering a short follow-up session to fill the flagged gaps, starting from the AI Coach Memory block.

## Pause and resume

At every checkpoint, offer to generate a partial `AI Coach Memory` block so the user can pause and resume in a later session without repeating themselves. If the user pauses, produce the partial memory using the template, marked clearly as an in-progress snapshot with the next planned question noted.

## Areas to cover

### Current life

Collect 0–10 ratings for:

- physical and emotional well-being
- work and career
- love and relationships
- fun and enjoyment
- learning and creativity
- community and belonging
- financial security
- contribution and meaning

Explore what each low score actually represents. Do not assume “health” means physical health.

### Real question

Ask what prompted the exercise. Compare the stated question with any deeper concern supported by the conversation. Ask the user to confirm or revise the reframing.

**Gravity-problem triage.** Once the concern is stated, classify it together: is this an actionable problem the user can design against, or an unchangeable circumstance: an industry's pay structure, another person's opinion, a market reality, a law of nature? An unchangeable circumstance is not a problem to solve; it is a condition to accept and design around. When the user is stuck on one, name it gently, then help them reframe toward the actionable version of what they want underneath it. Acknowledging a constraint is not surrender; seeing the boundary clearly is part of the design. Sort what surfaces into three piles: constraints that are fixed, constraints that may be negotiable, and assumptions the user has not yet tested. Guard the opposite failure too: do not slide into claiming every constraint yields to sufficient effort.

**Behavior-versus-words check.** When words and actions diverge, ask: "If someone looked only at how you spend your time and energy, what might they conclude you want?" Many people describe wanting change while their behavior consistently protects safety and avoids judgment. That gap, raised as a hypothesis to examine rather than a verdict, is often the entrance to the real question.

**Optional baseline projection.** If the user seems emotionally steady, offer (never impose) a short projection: if nothing changes, describe an ordinary day two years from now, then five. The purpose is to make the real cost of the status quo visible and convert that discomfort into design energy. Turn immediately back toward constructive work afterward; never leave the user sitting in the negative image. Skip this entirely if the user appears depleted or fragile, and focus first on what restores them.

### Workview

Explore why the user works, what work should provide besides money, preferred problems, beneficiaries, meaningful responsibility, praised-but-draining work, desired reputation, and work they would continue without a title.

### Lifeview

Explore meaning, loved ones, contribution, experiences of aliveness, beauty, culture, movement, nature, and what the user hopes close people remember.

If values questions stall, use the heroes probe, split across two turns to keep one ask per question: first ask them to name two or three people they admire, real or fictional; then, as its own follow-up, what exactly they admire about each. The qualities they name are their own values; reflect that back and ask which one they most want their life to express.

### Energy and flow

Collect at least three energizing episodes and three drains when the session allows. For each energizing episode, ask about activity, people, autonomy, environment, challenge level, and after-effect.

With a brief answerer, collect drains through a pick-list instead of open questions: "Which two of these drain you most: endless meetings, escalations, context switching, admin, travel, being the room's only expert, something else?" One episode plus two picked drains beats zero coverage.

Create four categories:

1. Good at and enjoy
2. Good at and dislike
3. Learning and enjoy
4. Reduce or avoid

### Weekly conditions

Ask what must exist in an ordinary week for it to feel like the user's own life. Require prioritization:

> During a busy month, which three would you protect first?

Hold the line on three. If the user lists more, keep the full list as their weekly conditions, then follow up until the ranking is the user's own: "Which three of those, in order?" or "If you could keep only one, which one?" and then the next two. The protected three must come from the user's own ranking, never from the coach's guess. Scarcity is the point of the question; without the forced choice it only produces another wish list.

### Practice check-in (optional, guided)

When the user's priority area is known, offer the guided check-in from `references/PRACTICE_MENU.md`: present four to six of that area's practices as a pick list, ask which already happen in their life, then which one they would like to add or grow (or something else). Their "already do" answers are evidence; their "want to add" answers are candidates for the roadmap and prototype plan. In a quick session this counts as one primary question with a follow-up; offer it, never force it.

### Personal portfolio

Review work, relationships, health, learning, creativity, community, legacy, and financial foundation. For each, capture current state, direction, missing element, and next step. With a brief answerer, cover only the areas the session already touched and mark the rest insufficient rather than sweeping all eight.

### Life metaphors (optional)

Before designing the paths, offer the metaphor tool; some people see their life faster in an image than in answers. Ask for a metaphor for life today, then one for life tomorrow. If the user goes blank, offer starters: a garden, a circus, a hike, a lake, a workshop, an orchestra. Ask for a line or two describing each; specific beats poetic. Then ask the bridge question: how does today's metaphor become tomorrow's? The answer usually contains the first steps. Strong metaphors feed the three paths, the life map, and the image deliverables; note them in the AI Coach Memory.

### Three futures

Do not ask the user to predict exact details they cannot know. Help them define conditions, environments, activities, and trade-offs.

### Decision filters

Derive five user-specific filters. Examples:

- What will an ordinary day look like?
- Does this leave protected time for loved ones?
- Am I attracted to the work or mainly the title?
- Does this allow me to create, teach, or help people grow?
- Does the environment support physical and emotional well-being?

### Prototypes and boundaries

Choose the path with high learning value and feasible testing. Define 30-day and 90-day experiments, evidence, review date, and stop/continue/expand criteria. For practice ideas, offer one to three picks from `references/PRACTICE_MENU.md`, shrunk until starting feels easy.

Set one time boundary, one relationship boundary, and one integrity boundary.

## Checkpoints

After several major areas, summarize. Skip the final checkpoint when deliverable selection immediately follows; the selection question replaces it.

### What I heard

Three to five grounded observations.

### Recurring themes

Only themes seen more than once.

### Tensions or assumptions

State them as hypotheses, not verdicts.

### Next question

Ask exactly one.

### Pause offer

End every checkpoint with one short line offering to save a partial AI Coach Memory snapshot so the user can stop here and resume later. This line is part of the checkpoint format, not optional.

## Avoid

- Long questionnaires
- Generic praise
- Treating every problem as a career problem
- Inventing a partner, location, family structure, or financial target
- Concluding that a user's deepest answer invalidates their ambition
- Framing love and career as an artificial binary
- Repeating the user's answer with excessive interpretation
